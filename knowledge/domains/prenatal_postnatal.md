# Domain 9 — Pre & Postnatal Training
**Experts: Brianna Battles · Jessie Mundell**

---

## Overview

Pregnancy and postpartum are not medical conditions — they are life phases with specific physiological realities that require intelligent programming modification. The AI coach does not restrict movement out of fear. It applies evidence-based frameworks to keep clients active safely throughout pregnancy and return them to full training systematically postpartum.

> **Critical Rule:** The 6-week OB clearance is a medical baseline, not a return-to-sport clearance. A physician who says "you're cleared" at 6 weeks is assessing wound healing — not pelvic floor function, load tolerance, or readiness for high-impact or high-load training. These are fundamentally different assessments.

---

### Principle: Trimester-Based Training Framework (Battles)

| Phase | Training Approach |
|---|---|
| First Trimester | Fatigue and nausea often dominate. Maintain current activity levels — no need for dramatic modifications for most exercises in early pregnancy. Training continuity over performance. Avoid supine positions only if they cause symptoms (blanket restriction is outdated). Monitor for dizziness, pelvic girdle pain, or disproportionate shortness of breath. |
| Second Trimester | Often the most comfortable training period — energy returns. Begin progressive modification as belly grows: use incline bench instead of flat press, reduce load on exercises with high intra-abdominal pressure (heavy deadlifts, loaded squats with breath-holding). Pelvic floor awareness becomes important. |
| Third Trimester | Load management is primary. Volume and intensity typically decrease. Movement quality and body awareness take priority. Walking, swimming, modified strength work, and mobility are generally well-tolerated throughout. Many clients continue training to near-term with appropriate modification. |

**Red Flag Symptoms — Refer Immediately:**
Vaginal bleeding or fluid leakage · Severe shortness of breath · Chest pain · Significant pelvic girdle pain · Calf pain or swelling (DVT risk) · Decreased fetal movement

These require immediate medical attention — not modification.

### Coaching Application
Do not restrict movement out of fear. The evidence supports maintaining training through pregnancy with appropriate modifications. When in doubt: keep the client moving, reduce intensity, and monitor for symptoms. Ask at every check-in: "Any symptoms during exercise this week?"

### Signals
Use when: client discloses pregnancy at any stage, asks about training while pregnant, or presents with prenatal symptoms during exercise.

### Principle: 4-Phase Postpartum Return-to-Sport (Battles)

| Phase | Timeline | Focus |
|---|---|---|
| Phase 1 | Weeks 1–6 | Restore breathing mechanics and diaphragmatic function. Reconnect with pelvic floor (gentle activation and relaxation). Walking. Rest. This is not training — it is foundation rebuilding. No loading the pelvic floor with high-impact activities or heavy lifting. |
| Phase 2 | Weeks 6–12 | Progressive loading begins. Bodyweight exercises. Low-impact conditioning. Assess for symptoms at every session: leaking (urine or gas), pelvic heaviness, prolapse symptoms, or pain. Any of these = pelvic floor PT referral before advancing. |
| Phase 3 | Weeks 12–20 | Return to loaded strength work. Progressive overload resumes. Impact work (jogging) introduced when symptom-free. Core and pelvic floor load tolerance is tested and built progressively. |
| Phase 4 | Month 5+ | Return to full sport-specific activities, running, CrossFit-style training, heavy lifting. Timeline varies significantly based on birth type, complications, breastfeeding status, and sleep. |

### Coaching Application
When a postpartum client wants to return to training: assess symptoms first, not timeline. "Are you experiencing any leaking, pelvic heaviness, or pain during movement?" drives the phase assignment — not weeks since delivery. A client with symptoms at week 10 is still in Phase 1 protocol regardless of the calendar.

### Signals
Use when: postpartum client asks about returning to training, expresses urgency to "get back to normal," or presents within the first year postpartum.

### Principle: C-Section Considerations
Cesarean section is major abdominal surgery. The anterior abdominal wall requires 12–16 weeks minimum before significant loading. Do not apply the same return timeline to C-section clients that applies to vaginal birth clients. The 6-week clearance from an OB after C-section is especially insufficient as a return-to-training benchmark.

### Coaching Application
Always ask about birth type before assigning any postpartum protocol. C-section clients begin Phase 3 (loaded strength) no earlier than weeks 12–16. Scar tissue mobilization and abdominal reconnection work is a prerequisite.

---

### Principle: Pelvic Floor Is Not All Kegels (Mundell)
The pelvic floor requires both activation AND relaxation. A hypertonic (too tight) pelvic floor produces symptoms similar to a weak one: leaking, pelvic pain, difficulty with penetration. A pelvic floor PT can differentiate — kegels are contraindicated for the hypertonic client.

Any postpartum client presenting with leaking, pelvic heaviness, prolapse symptoms, or pain during exercise should be referred to a pelvic floor PT before advancing in protocol.

### Coaching Application
Do not assume every postpartum client needs more kegels. The symptom presentation is the same for both hypertonic and hypotonic pelvic floors. Referral to a pelvic floor PT is the correct action — not a default strengthening prescription.

### Signals
Use when: postpartum client reports leaking, pelvic heaviness, pain during exercise, or difficulty with physical intimacy.

---

### Principle: Postpartum Body Image and Identity (Mundell)
Many women experience significant distress about their postpartum body. The AI coach approaches this with compassion — framing the postpartum body as one that accomplished something extraordinary and is in a rebuilding phase — not a broken thing to be fixed as quickly as possible.

The urgency to "bounce back" is a cultural narrative that often drives dangerous return-to-training timelines and harmful relationships with food and exercise postpartum.

### Coaching Application
Validate the experience. Reframe the frame. "Your body did something extraordinary. Right now it's rebuilding. That takes time and it's worth doing right." Never reinforce urgency to return to a pre-pregnancy body or timeline. The client who returns gradually and intelligently will achieve better long-term outcomes than the one who rushed.

---

### Principle: Breastfeeding Considerations
Prolactin elevation during breastfeeding suppresses estrogen — similar in some physiological respects to perimenopause. Breastfeeding clients may experience: elevated joint laxity (relaxin lingers), temporarily reduced bone density, higher caloric needs (300–500 additional kcal/day above pre-pregnancy maintenance), and potential for RED-S if intake is inadequate.

### Coaching Application
For breastfeeding clients: increase caloric and protein targets (not reduce them for fat loss). Monitor for underfueling symptoms. High-impact and high-intensity training is appropriate only once pelvic floor and core load tolerance are established — not based on "feeling ready."

### Signals
Use when: postpartum client is breastfeeding and asking about fat loss, caloric restriction, or returning to high-intensity training.

---

### Principle: Postpartum Sleep Context
New parents are chronically sleep-deprived. Training load expectations must reflect this reality. A postpartum client with a newborn who completes 3 short sessions per week is not underperforming — she is doing exceptionally well given her recovery context.

### Coaching Application
Calibrate expectations without eliminating standards. "3 sessions this week, even if they're short — that's the goal. Not because it's less than you're capable of, but because it's what your recovery context supports right now." Never compare postpartum performance to pre-pregnancy benchmarks.

### Signals
Use when: postpartum client is in the first 6–12 months, has a newborn, reports poor sleep, or expresses discouragement about performance.
